Another upshot of the Markov chain approach to state expectancies: the closed-form solution for expected future state visits, which (maybe?) supports a simple heuristic for bullpen and workload planning. 


**Hypothetical**
Consider the following scenario. You are the home team, and you are leading 2-1 with nobody out in the top of the sixth inning. Your starter has just hit traffic—a double followed by a quick walk—and is suddenly laboring. Unfortunately, your bullpen is gassed, with one more game (not including this one) remaining until your next off-day. You have your two highest-leverage, "circle-of-trust" (COT) relievers ostensibly available—however in light of heavy workloads, their usage today would preclude usage tomorrow. You can use them today and go for the win, but doing so may leave you with less-trusted pitchers on the hook for potential big innings tomorrow. How should you play it? All else equal, should you push the chips in on today's game to protect your lead? 

**Markov Chain Win Probability Revisited**
It's far from a perfect methodology, but I think the Markov chain formulation offers us a tidy, simple heuristic for answering these questions in a principled, data-driven manner. Recall from [before](https://isaackleislemurphy.github.io/posts/2026/09/post-1-markov-chain-state-expectancies) that, given a particular starting (transient) state, we have a closed-form solution for the expected number of rest-of-sequence visits to each of the other transient states prior to absorption. This solution is:

$$
(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1}
$$

where $$\mathbf{Q}$$ is the block subset of the transition matrix containing transition probabilities between transient states.

For a Markov assumption over an entire game—as is made to estimate win probability—this gives us an expected number of visits to each game state before the game ends. Since we have leverage for each such future game state, this also means that we can calculate the leverage index of each future state, and then determine the total number of expected visits to game states at or above a particular leverage threshold. In other words, you have the expected number of rest-of-game PAs where leverage is greater than 2.0, greater than 3.0, etc. 

**Return to Hypothetical**
With this framework, let's return to our hypothetical. Recall that the present game state is `12-:0` T6 with a home lead of +1. As derived from the [state expectancies explorer](https://ikm-state-expectancies.streamlit.app/), the hypothetical home team still maintains roughly a 53% chance of winning. However, with two on and nobody out, the run expectancy is 1.5 runs—it's a big spot, and thus the leverage index is roughly 3.0. 

On this leverage alone, there's a colorable—and certainly defensible case—for burning one of the COT relievers now. However, the opportunity cost of such usage isn't clear—at least until you consider the expected rest-of-game leverage visits above. Solving for $$(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1}$$ from above, we can estimate that, over the rest of this game, including the upcoming PA, there are:

* An expected 7.8 PAs with a leverage index of 2.0 or greater. 
* An expected 4.9 PAs with a leverage index of 3.0 or greater. 
* An expected 0.8 PAs with a leverage index of 4.0 or greater. 

In other words, we can expect roughly another 3 PAs in the [2.0, 3.0] leverage bracket; another 4 PAs in the [3.0, 4.0] bracket; and almost one PA where leverage is greater than 4.0.

By contrast, tomorrow's game (i.e., examining $$(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1}$$ from `---:0` T1 with a home lead of zero) has:

* An expected 4.1 PAs with a leverage index of 2.0 or greater. 
* An expected 1.4 PAs with a leverage index of 3.0 or greater. 
* An expected 0.5 PAs with a leverage index of 4.0 or greater. 

That's good for roughly 2.5 PAs in the [2.0, 3.0] bracket and one in the [3.0, 4.0] bracket.

So as these expectations (coarsely) show, there are more expected high-leverage spots remaining today than there are tomorrow. It might make sense to use one, if not both of your COT arms today. If 2.0 is your cutoff for meaningful leverage (not unreasonable given that most capital-C Closers run `gmLI` values [at or just shy of 2.0](https://www.fangraphs.com/leaders/major-league?pos=all&lg=all&qual=y&type=3&season=2026&month=0&season1=2026&ind=0&stats=rel&sortcol=9&sortdir=default&pagenum=1)), then the napkin math here says there are nearly twice as many expected meaningful-leverage PAs as there are tomorrow. Or if 3.0 is your threshold, then there are almost five expected high-leverage PAs today—likely requiring both pitchers, since the Markov formulation does _not_ guarantee these PAs are contiguous—instead of 1.4 tomorrow. Even if you take the conservative route and apportion tomorrow's anticipated leverage to one of the COT guys (thereby keeping him down today), the other reliever should certainly pitch today. Otherwise, he might be waiting for additional leverage that may not materialize. 


**Discussion**
Of course, this quasi-breakeven approach is merely a heuristic—there are many valid criticisms to be leveled at it. For one, there are no guarantees that the expected leverage PAs are consecutive such that one pitcher could cover them cleanly. Perhaps some of them are expected in the current inning, whereas others are expected in the eighth or ninth inning—in this way, a single "PA" deemed high-leverage could actually be spread across multiple innings and thus outside the coverage window of a single reliever. The $$(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1}$$ makes no guarantees about proximity or convenience. 

Furthermore, the leverage and win probabilities herein are derived from population-level transitions and behaviors—they do not account for specific matchups, platoon tendencies, or lineup construction. Stronger or weaker pockets of the lineup may steer the future state visits toward or away from more competitive game states, effectively upweighting or downweighting the expected future leverage. For example, an uncharacteristically strong 6-9 may result in more traffic in one or two innings, which may in turn upweight the number of higher-leverage state visits. Alternatively, using your COT reliever right now may "stamp out" some future leverage opportunities by slamming the door and maximizing the odds of a clean 7th inning and a 1+ run lead for the rest of the bullpen after he exits. In the same way that leverage index for a specific matchup might decouple from population-level transitions—and in turn, from win probabilities—future game state visits may similarly decouple, with their knock-on effects for other future states. 

That's all to say that, when faced with the scenario presented in this post, the proper solution is to run your options through a proper matchup and game simulator, such as that provided by [FanGraphs](https://fangraphs.com/leaders/major-league?pos=all&lg=all&qual=y&type=8&season=2026&month=0&season1=2026&ind=0&stats=rel). But as a simple heuristic—a quasi-breakeven that can be employed in the heat of the moment—I think you could do a lot worse. By calculating the number of high-leverage state visits for the rest of a game and comparing that to the next day's expectations, you can begin to earmark reliever usage or know what you're giving up when you push the chips in. To the extent that you're (i) inclined to put your better relievers where the leverage is and (ii) willing to treat the expected number of high-leverage PAs as a reasonable proxy for the value of preserving a reliever, then this method offers a rough way to price opportunity cost.
