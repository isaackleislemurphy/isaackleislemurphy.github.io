## 1.) Introduction

Since roughly my sophomore year of high school, I've thought it was funny to have (i) pedestrian, drive-time sports talk radio football takes generally but (ii) highly-specific, inside-baseball takes on special teams play. There’s whimsy in saying “run more Power Right” while simultaneously claiming that if modern punt/no punt decision-making is already too conservative, then overuse of the turnover punt only exacerbates this sub-optimality.

In service of the latter half of this bit, this post introduces a hierarchical, Gaussian-process forecasting framework for basic latent placekicker skills. At its core, this proposed model is simple: it's really just three classifiers—a block probability, a distance classifier, and an accuracy classifier—that get stitched together to predict overall field goal success. In so doing, its architecture provides for: (i) decomposition of kicking skill into "leg" (i.e. the ability to kick for distance) and "accuracy" (i.e. the ability to aim it between the uprights); (ii) time-varying player effects that can be forecasted forward to create placekicker projections; and (iii) fixed effects that more rigorously distinguish basic environmental factors from kicker talent. 

And while, in the grand scheme of things, this model is ultimately pretty simple, I'm sufficiently pleased with how it turned out. Moreover, I'd like to think that it charts a course towards tracking-based approaches—for example, ability to decompose additional latent skills or derive FG probs by modeling a kicker's "spray" at the goalpost, and identifying misses/makes. So with that, let’s get into the model. 

## 2.) Generative Model

#### A.) Notation

To define the model, let:

***I.) Indices***

- $$n=0, \ldots, N - 1$$ index observed kick attempts in the dataset—blocked, missed, or successful.
    - $$E[n] \in \{0, 1\}$$ be an indicator of whether kick $$n$$ is an extra point ($$E[n] = 1$$) or a regular field goal ($$E[n] = 0$$).
- $$d=0, \ldots, D$$ index discrete kick distances. For example, with a ten yard end zone and an assumed seven-yard hold, $$d=0$$ would correspond to a seventeen yard kick attempt.
    - $$d[n]$$ index the distance of kick $$n$$.
- $$k=0, \ldots, K-1$$ index unique kickers in the dataset.
- $$s = 0, \ldots, S - 1$$ index unique seasons in the dataset.
- $$t_k[s] \leq S - 1$$ index an individual kicker’s timesteps in the dataset. For example, if kicker $$k'$$ plays his rookie season in season $$s' = 4$$, then we would have $$t_{k'}[s' = 4] = 0$$.
- $$a_k[s]$$ describe kicker $$k$$’s age in season $$s$$ (pinned to September 1st of the league year).

***II.) Design Matrix / Fixed Effects***

- $$\mathbf{x}_n \in \mathbb{R}^M$$ be a vector of fixed effects—typically pertaining to the kicking environment (*e.g.*, outdoor/indoor, K-ball/not, etc.) that may affect the kick. *The specific features that comprise $$\mathbf{x}_n$$ are described in greater detail later.*

***III.) Responses / Observed Random Variables***

- $$y_n^{(\text{Block})} \in \{0, 1\}$$ denote whether kick attempt $$n$$ was blocked $$(y_n^{(\text{Block})} = 1)$$ or not blocked $$(y_n^{(\text{Block})} = 0)$$.
- $$y_n^{(\text{Leg})}  \in \{0, 1\}$$ denote whether kick attempt $$n$$, conditional on being unblocked, had sufficient distance (”leg”). Formally, this is $$y_n^{(\text{Leg})} | \;y_n^{(\text{Block})} = 0$$, but for readability I’ll refer to it as $$y_n^{(\text{Leg})}$$.
- $$y_n^{(\text{Acc.})}  \in \{0, 1\}$$ denote whether kick attempt $$n$$, conditional on being unblocked, was on target. Again, this is technically $$y_n^{(\text{Acc.})} | \;y_n^{(\text{Block})} = 0$$, but for readability notation is once again compressed.

#### B.) Data

All data for this project were ingested via `nflreadpy`, which is pointed at the `nflverse-data` data repository. Training windows spanned eight seasons (*e.g.*, 2015-2022, 2018-2025)—with additional compute or time, this could ostensibly be expanded.

#### C.) Model Architecture

*In the following subsections, I’ll describe the model in “building blocks” (not to be confused with blocked kicks), which loosely reflect my workflow in building a model from the ground up. That is, I start with a simplified version of Block 1, and get it to sample cleanly. Then I iterate (stumble?) towards the architecture I want for the block, and once that’s in a good place, I start on the second building block.*

*By presenting the model in this way, hopefully readers can see my stepwise iteration and model crescendo. My early passes at the first building block are merely a baby, baseline GLM; building blocks one through five collectively should better approximate the data generating process.* 

**Building Block 1: Distance-Related Decay Function**

**Setup**

First—and most central to the model—I use a latent GP to model block, leg, and accuracy probabilities as a function of distance. Using an exponentiated quadratic kernel $$\mathcal{K}$$ for each decay curve, this is:

$$
\begin{align}
f^{(\text{Block})} &\sim \text{GP}\left(\mu^{(\text{Block})}(d), \mathcal{K}^{(\text{Block})}(d, d')\right)
\\
f^{(\text{Leg})} &\sim \text{GP}\left(\mu^{(\text{Leg})}(d), \mathcal{K}^{(\text{Leg})}(d, d')\right)
\\
f^{(\text{Acc.})} &\sim \text{GP}\left(\mu^{(\text{Acc.})}(d), \mathcal{K}^{(\text{Acc.})}(d, d')\right)
\end{align}
$$

**Kernels**

Of course, exact GPs would be a bear here, so I instead use Hilbert space approximations. To this end, kick distances are mapped onto a grid that spans $$[-1, 1]$$, with each grid point representing one additional yard of kicking distance. Priors on the length-scale parameters were deliberately chosen (*see* `pm.find_constrained_prior`) to discourage length scales that were either narrower than the grid spacing or wider than the grid entirely (in addition to just making sense, this meaningfully improved sampling relative to a less-informed prior). Meanwhile, HSGP hyperparameters (25 basis functions; $$C = 5$$) were set on the advice of [Riutort-Mayol et al](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.11408).

**Mean Functions**

Finally, mean functions were:

$$
\mu_d^{(\text{Leg})} = \mu_d^{(\text{Acc.})} = -\mu_d^{(\text{Block})} = -5. 
$$

In other words, we assume the kick will be blocked and/or missed at a given distance until the data instructs otherwise. Hacky as it may be, this mean-setting strategy was my back-door attempt to correct for the selection bias of field goal attempts. Coaches only attempt the kicks they think they can make (or at least, those that are above the probability required to satisfy a win probability breakeven); accordingly, many of the misses from hopeless kicks aren’t present in our dataset, because they were never attempted. Thus, the strongly negative prior mean encourages the GP to treat the kick as hopeless until there is enough data to think otherwise. In turn, commonly observed kick distances—roughly those between $$17$$ and $$55$$ yards—should swamp this prior and pull the decay curve north of $$-5$$. However, longer kicks (*e.g.*, distances of $$60$$+ or even $$65+$$), which are less densely observed, will have less data to overwhelm the prior. Accordingly, these distances will remain closer to the pessimistic prior, and the latent decay curve will be “dragged down” as distance increases. 

Of course, this strategy is not a truly rigorous correction for the selection bias in kick attempts—some form of (i) inverse probability weighting, or (ii) a dual-likelihood architecture where each latent kick success mean also pointed to an attempt/no attempt Bernoulli likelihood (i.e. a selection function) would address this dynamic more thoughtfully. However, inverse probability weighting doesn’t always square neatly with Bayesian data generation, and a selection function would likely make the model computationally unwieldy. So I settled for this backdoor mean approach—invariably, the correction will still be sensitive to the choice of prior, but my hope is that it’s at least directionally correct. 

**Building Block 2: Fixed Environmental Effects**

Fixed effects—mainly designed to account for environment and rules changes—are more straightforward. The vector $$\mathbf{x}_n$$ contains the following variables:

**Table I, Fixed Effects**

| Feature | Domain | Description/Justification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `extra_point_attempt` | {0, 1} | Whether or not the kick was part of an extra point attempt. While extra point distance should already be captured via building block one, defensive rush quality (*e.g.*, less effort?) may differ, or familiarity with the specific distance may alter the probabilities. |
| `outdoors`  | {0, 1} | Whether or not the kick took place outdoors. Outdoor kicks may be subject to trickier weather or atmospheric conditions, reducing the likelihood of kick success.  |
| `grass` | {0, 1} | Whether or not the kick occurred on grass. Playing surface may impact a kicker’s footing, windup, or ability to create torque around the ball.  |
| `altitude` | {0, 1} | Whether or not the game was played at altitude, namely in Denver or Mexico City. Prime Matt Prater is the justification here—the ball soars at altitude.  |
| `k_ball` | {0, 1} | Whether or not the kick occurred in or after the 2025 league year. Beginning in 2025, the NFL began to permit special “K-Balls” used exclusively for kicking play. To the extent these balls have different ball flight properties, we’d want the model to pick up on them.  |

Within the model, this $$\mathbf{x}_n$$ term was dotted with a linear matrix

$$
\begin{align}
\mathbf{\beta} \sim \mathcal{M}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}_5, \mathbf{I}_3) \in\mathbb{R}^{5\times 3}
\end{align}
$$

for a contribution of $$\mathbf{x}_n^T \beta$$ to the log-odds of each of the block, leg, and accuracy outcome components. 

Obviously, these effects are far too simple, and I’ve left a lot of feature engineering to be desired. For one, I’m not making full use of the descriptive meteorological information provided by `nflreadpy`, which reports general temperature and wind conditions for a game. Accordingly, I may not be sufficiently crediting makes in swirling conditions, or debiting distance in hot conditions. Moreover, stadiums as a whole likely deserve their own set of effects—kicking on the lakefront at Soldier Field is probably a whole different animal than kicking on the lakefront at Huntington Bank. 

So the aforementioned improvements (and possibly separate stadium effects) are noted, and should be free points for improvement whenever I dust this model off next. But for now, I think we can live with these coarser environmental effects—with a week before classes resumed, I prioritized building out the kicker-specific latent skill trajectories.

Finally, it bears mention that I did not have access to information about the horizontal placement (i.e. left hash / right hash / between the hashes) of the kick—this information would surely be useful insofar as it affects the geometry of the uprights relative to the kicker. 

**Building Block 3: Time-Varying Kicker Effects**

A logical next step was adding in time-varying kicker effects for leg and accuracy. Here, I spun up a standard AR(1) process, i.e.

$$
\begin{align}
\rho^{(\text{Leg/Acc.})} & \sim 0.98 \cdot \text{Beta}(\alpha=3, \beta=2)\\
\sigma_0^{(\text{Leg/Acc.})} &\sim \mathcal{N}^+(0.5) \\ 
\epsilon_{p, t}^{(\text{Leg/Acc.})} &\sim \mathcal{N}\left(0, \sigma_0^{(\text{Leg/Acc.})^2}
\cdot (1 - \rho^{(\text{Leg/Acc.})^2})\right)
\\
\xi_{p, t=0}^{(\text{Leg/Acc.})} &\sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_0^{(\text{Leg/Acc.})^2})\\
\xi_{p, t + 1}^{(\text{Leg/Acc.})} 
&= \rho^{(\text{Leg/Acc.})} \cdot \xi_{p, t }^{(\text{Leg/Acc.})}
+ \epsilon_{p, t}^{(\text{Leg/Acc.})}  
\end{align}
$$

Longer term, I’d want to amend this to something more Linear Dynamical System-ish (I’d probably start with a Kalman filter) so that latent skills could evolve jointly—the AR(1) setup here precludes the model from learning covariances across skills, both in terms of correlated innovations and “cross-skill” autoregression. But getting those to sample nicely can get ugly fast, so I figured it sufficient to start with just the compartmentalized AR(1)s. 

For those who prefer reading Stan/PyMC models directly, the full model is linked [here](https://github.com/isaackleislemurphy/field-goal-probability/blob/28774cf9a5a579829cec6545a6ecb70e51c083b6/model/model.py#L13-L316)—note that `pm.AR(...)` is centered, so I reparameterized it manually. 

**Building Block 4: Kicker Age Effects**

Finally, to account for the effect of age on kicking skill, I attempted to build out aging curves for both the leg and accuracy components. Hoping to build towards something more flexible, I initially spun up linear terms—it’s described in more detail below, but the leg skill unsurprisingly declined sharply with age, while accuracy increased. I then attempted to convert these to GPs and (convexity-constrained) B-splines. This is where it got frustrating—the GPs largely replicated the linear fits (within the observed age ranges), and the convex-enforced B-spline terms struggled mightily to sample (note that I was not exponentiating, but rather cumulatively-summing Half-Normals). So, begrudgingly, I reverted back to the linear terms, ultimately going with linear aging that looked like:

$$
\begin{align}
f^{(\text{Leg/Acc.})}(a) &:= \theta^{(\text{Leg/Acc.})}\cdot
\left(
\frac{\min(\max(a, 21), 41)}{41 - 21}\right) \\
\theta^{(\text{Leg/Acc.})} &\sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.5^2)
\end{align}
$$

Of all the shortcomings with this model, this aging curve is the component that gives me the most pause. On one hand, allowing accuracy to increase with age feels like walking into the trap of survivorship bias, for which this model makes no correction. But on the other, perhaps it’s not wholly implausible that kickers don’t lose accuracy over time? Perhaps the touch remains (or even marginally improves with additional repetitions), and it’s the loss of power and force (i.e. the sharper decline in the leg skill) that sinks kickers. 

That’s all to say—I’m torn on this one, and there’s a good chance I revise my position when I come back to this next. I probably should also spend more time trying to get the GP/B-Spline to sample, and tease out which, if any, kickers are distorting the accuracy curve the most. But for the time being, I ultimately left the linear aging in, because its inclusion marginally improved ELPD values and out-of-sample log-likelihoods.

**Building Block 5: Likelihoods**

Incorporating one through four above, the full model comes together as follows. Note that the block probability submodel just follows a generic population curve, plus fixed effects; the leg and accuracy components include the full suite of aging and AR(1) effects. 

$$
\begin{align}

\log P (y_n^{(\text{Block})} = 1)
&\propto \;\;

\mathbf{x}_n^T\beta_0 +

f^{(\text{Block})}(d[n]) 

\\

\log P(y_n^{(\text{Leg})} = 1)
&\propto \;\;

\mathbf{x}_n^T\beta_1 +

f^{(\text{Leg})}(d[n]) +

 

f^{(\text{Leg})}(a[n]) + 

\sum_{t'=0}^{t[n]}\xi^{(\text{Leg})}_{p[n], t'}

\\

\log P(y_n^{(\text{Acc.})} = 1)
&\propto \;\;

\mathbf{x}_n^T\beta_2 +

f^{(\text{Acc.})}(d[n]) +

 

f^{(\text{Acc.})}(a[n]) + 

\sum_{t'=0}^{t[n]}\xi^{(\text{Acc.})}_{p[n], t'}

\end{align}
$$

The overall success of the kick is then just a product of the three probabilities, i.e.  

$$
\begin{align}
P(\text{Success}) = (1 - P (y_n^{(\text{Block})} = 1) )\cdot P (y_n^{(\text{Leg})} = 1) \cdot P (y_n^{(\text{Acc.})} = 1)
\end{align}
$$

## Sampling / Parameter Estimation

Sampling was just brute-force `nutpie`, configured to 14 parallel chains, each with 10,000 tuning draws and 10,000 posterior draws. On a MacBook Pro, an eight-season model (*e.g.*, training on the 2018-2025 seasons)—even with a target acceptance rate of 0.95—took a little over an hour. As detailed in the following section, the model sampled decently well. Some observations and maneuvers that materially improved sampling include:

- Using a manual, non-centered parameterization for the AR(1) substantially improved posterior exploration—energy plots using the built-in `pm.AR()`, which implements a centered parameterization, were too narrow.
- A careful choice for the $$\rho \sim \text{Beta}(\cdot)$$ prior also prevented the autoregressive coefficients from getting stuck. I settled on a prior mean of $$0.6$$ since that seemed like a reasonable rate of year-over-year decay; however, I still wanted plenty of wiggle room on either end. A $$\text{Beta}(3, 2)$$ achieved this; the concave density over $$[0, 1]$$ also helped prevent $$\rho$$ from “bouncing” or otherwise getting stuck near the upper bound, as seemed to be the case when I tried $$\beta$$ parameters less than one (that still satisfied a prior mean of $$\approx 0.6$$).
- As noted above, careful priors over the length scale parameters of the Gaussian Process were also essential to acceptable sampling; I mostly stuck to [this advice](https://discourse.pymc.io/t/possible-ways-to-speed-up-spatio-temporal-gp-modeling/13127/5) for choosing priors that are coherent with the grid’s resolution.

**Posterior Predictive Checks**

Last up are the posterior predictive checks. Rather than repeatedly call the built-in `az.plot_ppc()`, which I don't find to be super helpful in categorical log-odds settings, I instead visualized my posterior predictive checks through the following posterior-predictive / calibration hybrid:

1. Bucket each observed kick attempt into five-yard buckets, i.e. `[15, 19]`, `[20, 24]`, `[25, 29]`, etc.
2. For each such bucket:
    1. Calculate the observed success rate.
    2. For each posterior draw, calculate that draw's predicted success rate within the bucket (i.e. the fraction of the bucket's kicks that the draw marks successful).
3. Using the per-draw success rates from (2b), take the median predicted rate, along with intervals spanning the central 90% and 95% of draws (plus the full min/max range). For each bucket, plot these against the observed success rate from (2a).

Ideally, the bands should "enclose" the observed rates, which is largely how things played out:

![pp-check-dist-2018_2025.png](Field%20Goal%20Probs%20Writeup%20Pt%20I/pp-check-dist-2018_2025.png)

That's encouraging—at least (i) with respect to the training data and (ii) as a coarse function of distance, the model seems capable of replicating the observed proportions.
