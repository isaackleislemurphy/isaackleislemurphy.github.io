Most public research websites present run expectancy, win probability, and leverage index based on observed historical rates. This post (a) outlines a Markov chain formulation of these state expectancies; (b) quickly validates that methodology; and (c) introduces a [UI](https://ikm-state-expectancies.streamlit.app/) to explore these expectancies and probabilities.

## I. Introduction

RE24, Win Probability, and Leverage Index are by now foundational concepts in modern baseball analysis. Popular public touch points for these metrics can be found on sites like [Greg Stoll](https://gregstoll.com/~gregstoll/baseball/stats.html#V.0.1.0.1.0.0), [Baseball Savant](https://baseballsavant.mlb.com/game-strategy-explorer?type=winexp&qs=%7B%22inning%22%3A9%2C%22half%22%3A%22Top%22%2C%22outs%22%3A0%2C%22balls%22%3A0%2C%22strikes%22%3A0%2C%22situation%22%3A%22%22%2C%22run_diff%22%3A0%2C%22runners%22%3A%7B%221b%22%3Afalse%2C%222b%22%3Afalse%2C%223b%22%3Afalse%7D%2C%22perspective%22%3A%22bat%22%7D), or [TangoTiger](https://tangotiger.net/we.html), which—at least as I understand them—rely on empirical rates and outcomes observed from each game state. In RL-speak, these calculations are conceptually analogous to every-visit Monte Carlo ("EVMC") value estimation. Given the volume of games played each season, the approach generally works well, though it can be vulnerable in small samples (*e.g.*, rare late-game scenarios).

I believe baseball can be well-approximated by a discrete-time Markov chain—even where its memorylessness and independence assumptions aren't strictly met—and that, where possible, a Markov framework should be used to estimate these quantities. Of course, there’s a tradeoff here: the Markov approach concedes some model misspecification (bias) in exchange for estimates that stabilize faster (lower variance). Among its advantages when it comes to state expectancies:

- The discrete-time Markov chain has nice properties. There are closed-form solutions for run expectancy, win probability, the expected time until an inning or game ends, and the expected number of visits to other states before a game concludes. *As I'll discuss in a future note, being able to estimate expected visits over the rest of a game lets us build out bullpen-planning heuristics.*
- Empirical EVMC estimates are only as good as the sample size for each state. For RE24, where each state is visited plenty of times over the course of a season or two, this is probably fine. But when states get more granular—as with win probability—sample size can become an issue. We might have to wait until we've seen 500 games play out from a `1-3:0`, down-by-two situation in the top of the ninth before we can trust the empirical win probability. By contrast, the transition probabilities underneath a Markov approach can be derived using information from all plays, rather than particularized traffic scenarios (*e.g.*, `1-3:0`). Accordingly, these transition probabilities may stabilize faster, allowing us to incorporate rule or run-environmental changes with greater alacrity.
- The Markov approach stitches together RE24, Win Probability, and Leverage Index in a unified and internally consistent way. Because each calculation is built iteratively on top of the last, they all trace back to the same ground truths. I find that compelling.

This post is pretty simple. It just offers up a Markov-implementation and a UI, for these classic RE24, Win Probability, and Leverage Index metrics, which to the best of my knowledge, isn’t really out there. Not looking to reinvent the wheel here—just implementing a basic Markov chain formulation of it all. 

## II. Absorbing-State Markov Chains Generally

As a quick recap, suppose you have a discrete-time Markov chain with both transient states and absorbing states. The transition matrix $$\mathbf{P}$$ can be blocked by transient/absorbing status, i.e.

$$
\mathbf{P} = 
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{Q} & \mathbf{R} \\
\mathbf{0} & \mathbf{I}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Here, $$\mathbf{Q}$$ governs transitions between transient states, while $$\mathbf{R}$$ governs transitions from transient states to absorbing states. $$\mathbf{I}$$ then enforces a self-loop on the absorbing states, and the lower left block is zero, as there is no way to transition from an absorbing state back to a transient state. Under this decomposition, we get the following closed-form properties:

**A.) Absorption Probabilities**

$$(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q} )^{-1}\mathbf{R}$$ gives us the absorption probabilities, conditional on starting in each transient state. That is, $$((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{R})_{i, j}$$ gives us the probability of ultimately ending up in (absorbing) state $$j$$ from starting (transient) state $$i$$.  *In a baseball/game strategy context, this might be our RE24 values (where absorbing states are `---:3` base-out states, coupled with the total runs scored in the inning). Or this might be our win probability values (where absorbing states are home-team-win and home-team-loss).* These probabilities will be the primary focus of this post.

**B.) Expected Transient State Visits**

$$(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q} )^{-1}$$ gives us the expected number of visits to each transient state prior to absorption, conditional on starting in a particular transient state. That is, $$(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}_{i, j}$$ tells us, conditional on starting in state $$i$$, how many times we should expect to visit state $$j$$ prior to absorption. *In a baseball/game strategy context, this might be useful for evaluating rest-of-game leverage and the opportunity cost of using a particular reliever now versus later.* Heuristics related to these expectations are the focus of a later post.

**C.) Expected Transitions to Absorption** 

$$(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{1}$$ gives us the expected time to absorption from a particular state. That is $$((\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{1})_i$$ tells us, conditional on starting in state $$i$$, the expected number of transitions until the Markov chain achieves a terminal state. *In a baseball/game strategy context, this may be useful for population-level batters-per-inning or batters-per-game heuristics.*

Nothing particularly new, fancy, or insightful there. For a more detailed explication of these quantities, I’ve always found [this link](https://www.math.umd.edu/~immortal/MATH401/book/ch_absorbing_markov_chains.pdf) quite helpful. 

## III. Inning-Level Markov Chains (RE24)

To calculate basic RE24, we define a Markov chain over an inning as follows

- **State Space:** The state space covers all combinations of (`is_runner_on_1B` , `is_runner_on_2B` , `is_runner_on_3B`, `outs`, `runs`). That is, it’s (i) the twenty four classic base-out states (*e.g.*, `---:0`, `1--:0`, …, `123:2`) cross-joined with runs scored so far; and (ii) an inning end state (`—--:3`) cross-joined with total runs in the inning.
    - Note that the state space is discretized by capping `runs` at 15; once a team exceeds 15 runs, we roll the inning and it automatically transitions to a base-out state of `---:3` with 15 runs.
- **Transitions:** Transitions are defined by combinations of (`base_out_state_pre`, `base_out_state_post`, `runs_scored_on_play`). I use basic empirical, big-league rates across 2017-2025 to define these probabilities, broken out by half innings (ideally to the slight advantage of hitting in one’s home ballpark). The timespan is something I need to revisit, however. I think there’s a colorable argument to restricting to 2023-2026 or 2024-2026 to better pick up on the effects of the pitch clock and/or new pickoff rules.

With these states, transitions, and associated probabilities defined, all that remains is to solve the closed-form equation in II.A. The rows of $$(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q} )^{-1}\mathbf{R}$$ corresponding to `(---:0, 0)`, `(1--:0, 0)`, `(12-:0, 0)` , …, `(123:2, 0)` are the relevant starting states, while the columns corresponding to `(---:3, 0)` , `(---:3, 1)` , …, `(---:3, 15)` are our absorbing states.

# IV. Game-Level Markov Chains (Win Probability)

The same transition construction—that is, transitions from `game_state_pre` to `(game_state_post, runs_scored_on_play)`—can also support a Markov assumption over an entire game. Specifically, the state space is as follows:

- **State Space**:
    - Transient State Space: The transient state space now consists of combinations of (`inning`, `half_inning`, `base_out_state`, `home_lead` ). Leads are likewise truncated between [-15, 15]; beyond that the game is presumed over, position-player pitching and all.
    - Absorbing State Space: Whereas the absorbing space in the RE24 formulation keyed on runs, the terminal states here are just: `home_win` or `home_loss`.
- **Transitions**: Transitions are defined identically to those above, i.e. combinations of (`base_out_state_pre`, `base_out_state_post`, `runs_scored_on_play`). Again, I use half-inning-wise empirical probabilities, so it’s really two sets of transition probabilities taking turns under the hood.

The real work here involves ensuring that the (`base_out_state_pre`, `base_out_state_post`, `runs_scored_on_play`) transitions, as well as their associated empirical probabilities, correctly traverse this much larger state space—there’s plenty of annoying extra-innings, walk-off, and impossible-journey logic to work through. But once the game transition matrix is constructed, it’s the same $$(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q} )^{-1}\mathbf{R}$$, albeit with a bigger matrix inversion. 

## V. Leverage Index

Once win probabilities are calculated, they can be stitched together with the transition probabilities to calculate leverage index—it’s plug-and-chug at this point. The main thing worth noting is that I have recycled [Tom Tango’s](https://tht.fangraphs.com/crucial-situations/) “leverage denominator” of `0.034` for average win probability change—I need to recalculate this at some point in the future, but this widely-accepted baseline should suffice for now. 

## VI. Validation

It would be irresponsible to present these calculations without any form of validation—what follows here is a cursory check to ensure the Markov chain approach captures modern run and win expectancies. Accordingly, this section is not so much a full-scale, train-versus-test pipeline evaluation. Rather, it’s a couple of quick-and-dirty checks designed to answer the questions: "if we just look at empirical base-out state transition rates—the nucleus of all the above transition probabilities—can we recreate the corresponding run and win environments over entire inning and game sequences?"

To the first objective, run environment, consider the following expected-versus-observed plots for rest-of-inning runs from each of the twelve base-out states. On the x-axis, I plot run expectancies (or related probabilities of a particular rest-of-inning run total) as predicted by the Markov model. On the y-axis, I have the corresponding observed means and rates in the big leagues in the Statcast era. An upwards arrow corresponds to expectancies for the top half of an inning; a downwards arrow corresponds to expectancies for the bottom.

**Figure 1, Expected vs. Observed Rest-of-Innings Runs, MLB 2017-2025**

![re24-check.png](/images/post-1/re24-calibration.png)

It’s evident that expected rest-of-inning run expectancies correlate closely with what actually played out. This suggests the empirically-derived Markov chain can sufficiently reproduce a big league run environment. 

Turning to win probability, my cursory evaluation scheme involved (i) taking the starting game state for each big league plate appearance between 2017-2025; (ii) joining the Markov-based win probability for each such plate appearance; and then (iii) performing basic calibration and log-likelihood checks. The calibration curve for these predictions was as follows:

**Figure 2, Markov Chain Win Probability Calibration Curve, MLB 2017-2025**

![markov-calibration-20.png](/images/post-1/win-prob-calibration.png)

It’s not perfect—there’s a very slight “S” shape—but certainly passable, particularly for such a simple model. Notably, this model scored better by negative log-likelihood over the Statcast era than did MLB’s own internal model (as provided in Baseball Savant):

**Table I, Win Probability NLL’s, MLB 2017-2025**

| Predictor | Neg. Log Likelihood |
| --- | --- |
| Markov Chain Probability | 0.2274 |
| MLB-Provided Win Probability | 0.2427 |

This tells us that if we take a random plate appearance between 2017 and 2025, the Markov chain approach would, on average, provide a better win probability prediction than its MLB-provided counterpart.
